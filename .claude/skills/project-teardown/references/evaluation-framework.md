# Evaluation Framework

Use this framework to make product and market judgments consistent without turning the teardown into a generic scorecard.

## Product thesis

Express the product in one falsifiable sentence: for a specific user, it produces a specific outcome through a particular approach, with a reason to choose it over alternatives.

Test whether the actual product, positioning, architecture, and feature set support the same thesis. Flag ambiguity and contradictions rather than inventing coherence.

## Benchmark selection

Choose comparison products by relevance to the user's job, not superficial similarity. Include:

- Direct alternatives serving the same user and job.
- Substitutes that solve the job differently.
- Best-in-class products for a critical sub-experience, such as onboarding or CLI ergonomics.
- Emerging approaches that could make the current premise obsolete.
- Domain-specific products, open-source projects, research systems, benchmarks, and practitioner workflows when recommending a specialized niche.

Record why each benchmark is relevant. Avoid declaring an overall winner from incomparable feature lists.

Do not recommend entering or specializing in a category after reviewing only general-purpose competitors. If credible category evidence cannot be gathered, label the strategic recommendation provisional and state the research or user validation required.

## Classification

Classify material capabilities, assumptions, or directions using one or more labels:

- **Old news:** Common, commoditized, declining, or based on superseded expectations. Explain whether this is merely table stakes or actively harmful.
- **Contradictory:** Conflicts with another feature, user group, promise, workflow, business model, or technical constraint.
- **Demand-misaligned:** Credible evidence suggests weak demand, unacceptable friction, or a mismatch with the intended audience. State evidence quality.
- **Ahead of the curve:** Delivers a meaningful outcome that relevant alternatives do not yet match. Distinguish novelty from durable advantage.
- **One change from ahead:** A concrete, feasible change could create meaningful differentiation. Name the change and what must be true for the advantage to hold.
- **Heading for a wall:** Current choices create a likely technical, adoption, safety, legal, platform, economic, or product dead end. Identify the trigger, time horizon when supportable, and escape routes.

Do not force every finding into one of these labels.

## Feature value

For each major feature, evaluate:

1. User job and frequency.
2. Outcome quality and reliability.
3. Fit with the product thesis.
4. Interaction or conflict with other features.
5. Differentiation versus table stakes.
6. Complexity and maintenance burden.
7. Risk, trust, privacy, safety, or legal cost.
8. Evidence of demand or the cheapest validation needed.

Recommend keeping, strengthening, changing, combining, de-emphasizing, removing, or investigating it. Never recommend removal solely because the reviewer personally would not use it.

## Best-in-class versus solo-builder reality

Hold quality expectations high while separating gaps into:

- Achievable through better choices and focused execution.
- Achievable with sustained solo effort but substantial opportunity cost.
- Dependent on scale, proprietary data, partnerships, certification, distribution, capital, or a team.
- Structurally unrealistic under the stated constraints.

Recommend where to match incumbents, where to narrow scope, and where an asymmetric approach offers a credible path to surpass them.

## Evidence discipline

For external strategic claims, record the source, publication or update date, access date, and direct relevance. Prefer official documentation, release notes, pricing, policies, public usage data, credible research, and repeated user evidence. Label weak or conflicting evidence.

Never present search-result frequency, social-media enthusiasm, one review, or the reviewer's intuition as established market demand.

Separate three questions that require different evidence:

1. **Capability:** Can this product or competitor perform the job?
2. **Differentiation:** Is the capability meaningfully uncommon or better?
3. **Demand:** Do the intended users care enough to adopt, switch, or pay?

Competitor documentation can support capability comparisons. It rarely proves demand by itself.
